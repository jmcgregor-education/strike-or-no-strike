Would That Pitch Be a Strike?

Extending “A Falling Pitch” · horizontally launched projectile model · individual work

The setup

A pitcher throws a fastball exactly level (no upward or downward angle). We will treat it as a horizontally launched projectile: gravity is the only force, there is no air resistance, and the ball travels all the way to the plate.

Release speed90 mph = 40.2 m/s (1 mph = 0.447 m/s)
Release height1.8 m above the ground
Distance to home plate18.44 m
Strike zone for this batter0.5 m (knees) to 1.1 m (chest) above the ground

Part 1: Predict before you calculate

By the time the ball reaches the plate, how far will gravity have pulled it down?

Explain your reasoning in a sentence or two.

Key: About 1 m (1.03 m). Students often guess very small because the ball is “so fast.” Speed sets how far the ball goes in the time available, not how far it falls in that time.

Part 2: Calculate by hand

Use the two-column method and the GUESS steps. Show your work on paper or in the box, then check each answer.

Horizontala = 0 m/s², vₓ = 40.2 m/s, Δx = 18.44 m
Verticala = −9.8 m/s², vᵢᵧ = 0 m/s, Δy = ?

Watch out: time is the only quantity both columns share. Find it in the column where you know the most.

Is it a strike? How do you know?

Key: t = 18.44 ÷ 40.2 ≈ 0.458 s. Δy = ½(−9.8)(0.458)² ≈ −1.03 m. Height = 1.8 − 1.03 ≈ 0.77 m, inside the 0.5 to 1.1 m zone, so a strike. Getting the height right unlocks the numbers in the simulator below.

Part 3: Stress-test the model

The simulator draws the flight of the pitch. Its numbers stay locked until you get the height in Part 2 right, so the simulator confirms your thinking instead of replacing it. Vertical scale is exaggerated.

3a. Solve by hand: what is the slowest pitch (in mph) that still reaches the plate at 0.5 m or higher, released from 1.8 m?

Key: Work backward: allowed drop = 1.8 − 0.5 = 1.3 m, so t = √(2(1.3) ÷ 9.8) = 0.515 s, so v = 18.44 ÷ 0.515 = 35.8 m/s ≈ 80 mph.

3b. Predict, then test: where does a 70 mph pitch from 1.8 m cross the plate?

Does that match how a 70 mph pitch behaves in a real game? What does that tell you about the model?

Key: 70 mph = 31.3 m/s, t = 0.589 s, drop = 1.70 m, height ≈ 0.10 m (nearly in the dirt). Real slow pitches do not bounce that early, which is the opening to Part 4: real pitchers use spin to fight gravity.

3c. Pitch duel. Choose your own speed (60 to 100 mph) and release height. Give them to your partner without your answer.

If your answers differed, whose work held the error and what was it?

Part 4: Is the model good enough?

Real data about pitching: air drag slows a real fastball by roughly 8 to 10 mph before it reaches the plate, backspin on a fastball gives a small lift force, and topspin on a curveball adds a downward force.

1. Without calculating, would drag make the real drop bigger or smaller than your Part 2 answer? Use the equations to explain what happens to flight time.

Key: Bigger. A slower ball takes longer to cover 18.44 m, and the longer the time, the more the vertical motion has to act (Δy depends on t²). Students who say “slower means less drop” are mixing up speed and time.

2. The model says gravity is the only force. Name two other forces that act on a real pitch and say which direction each pushes or pulls.

Key: Drag (opposite the motion, mostly horizontal), Magnus/spin force (up for backspin, down for topspin, sideways for sidespin).

3. Claim, evidence, reasoning. Is the horizontally launched projectile model good enough to decide whether a real 90 mph fastball is a strike? Use numbers from this activity.

Key: Strong answers cite the 0.77 m prediction and the 0.5 to 1.1 m zone, note that the margin to the bottom of the zone is only about 0.27 m while drag alone adds extra drop, and conclude the model is a good first approximation but not precise enough for close calls. Either claim is acceptable if the reasoning uses the numbers.

4. Aspire extension: pitchers actually release the ball about 1.7 m in front of the pitching rubber, so the real flight distance is about 16.7 m, not 18.44 m. Recalculate the height over the plate for a 90 mph pitch from 1.8 m.

Does fixing that assumption move the pitch toward the middle of the strike zone or toward the edge? What does this tell you about checking assumptions?

Key: t = 16.7 ÷ 40.2 = 0.415 s, drop = 0.84 m, height ≈ 0.96 m, closer to the top of the zone. One assumption can shift the answer more than the arithmetic does.
